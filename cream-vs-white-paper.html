Cream or white paper? It changes more than most authors think.

A practical LT Publishing guide to choosing cream or white paper for novels, nonfiction, illustrated books, and art titles—based on readability, tone, paper color, and the kind of object you want the book to be.

Paper choice is one of those production decisions that sounds minor until you hold two copies of the same book side by side and realise they are not the same book at all. The text block looks different. The tone of the cover shifts. The page feels brighter or calmer. Even the perceived weight of the prose changes. Cream versus white paper is not a cosmetic afterthought. It is part of the reading experience.

For many authors, the default question is simply, “Which option does KDP offer?” LT’s view is narrower and more useful: what kind of book is this, and what sort of page does it want? The right answer depends on genre, typography, trim size, image use, and the tone you want the finished object to carry.

Why paper colour matters at all

Paper is the background against which every other interior decision is judged. A body font that feels warm and elegant on cream can look brittle on bright white. A nonfiction interior that feels crisp on white can look sleepy on cream. Readers do not usually name paper colour as the reason a book feels inviting or fatiguing, but they respond to it all the same.

Cream stock generally softens the page. It lowers contrast slightly and often makes long stretches of reading feel easier on the eye, especially in fiction. White stock feels cleaner, brighter, and more contemporary. It is often the better choice when the book relies on diagrams, charts, photographs, or a very modern typographic voice.

When cream paper usually makes sense

  • Trade fiction. Novels, historical fiction, romance, literary fiction, and most narrative work often sit very comfortably on cream stock. It gives the page a little warmth and reduces the glare of a stark black-on-white text block.
  • Long reading sessions. If the book is meant to be read in bed, on a train, or over several hours at a time, cream often feels gentler and less clinical.
  • Traditional or atmospheric books. A Victorian romance, a rural literary novel, or anything with a slightly classic sensibility may feel more at home on cream than on a hard white page.

Cream is not automatically “higher end,” but it often helps a novel feel like a trade book rather than a report in paperback form.

When white paper is the stronger choice

  • Practical nonfiction. If the book uses headings, lists, callouts, tables, or workbook-style structure, white often supports that clarity better.
  • Illustrated interiors. Art books, manuals, cookbooks, and any title with photographs or diagrams usually want white paper because colour and tonal detail reproduce more cleanly against it.
  • Very modern design. Some contemporary nonfiction and commercial design-heavy titles benefit from the crispness of white stock.

White paper also tends to show the contrast in ornamental devices, grey tints, and image captions more aggressively. That can be useful or unpleasant depending on the book.

The paper choice has to work with the typography

This is where paper discussions often become too abstract. Cream versus white is not a vote on “warm” versus “clean.” It has to be considered alongside the actual interior design. A page set in a slightly dark serif at a compact size may become oppressive on white if the margins are tight. The same page might settle beautifully on cream. A spacious nonfiction layout with sans-serif subheads and tables may lose some of its snap on cream.

In other words, paper colour is not an isolated preference. It is part of the whole page system: typeface, leading, margins, trim, image use, and category.

LT Publishing’s rule of thumb: cream usually suits immersive reading books; white usually suits information-heavy or image-heavy books. But the real decision is made by looking at the interior as a whole, not by treating stock choice as a standalone checkbox.

What paper colour says about the book

Readers may not consciously register paper stock, but it contributes to the book’s tone. Cream often feels quieter, more traditional, and slightly more forgiving. White feels sharper, cleaner, and more contemporary. Neither is inherently better. They simply tell the eye different things before the reader has taken in a single paragraph.

If you are publishing a novel and want it to feel like a serious trade paperback, cream is often the safer starting point. If you are publishing a guide, workbook, business title, or image-led book, white is often the more rational one.

The short version

Paper colour is part of book design, not just production settings. Cream and white create different reading environments, and the right choice depends on the kind of book you are making. The question is not which stock is “better” in the abstract. It is which one helps this book look composed, readable, and fully itself.
