Book design & formatting

Ever wonder why some books have gorgeous interiors and others simply look like words on paper?

Professional formatting is usually the answer—but not in the shallow sense of “someone made the file work.” A strong interior makes the book feel composed, readable, and properly published. It does not look like a manuscript that happened to be poured into a trim size. It looks as though someone understood what kind of book it was and built the pages accordingly.

Authors often use the word formatting to mean one thing: getting a file through Amazon KDP without an error message. That matters, but it is only the technical floor. A professional interior is something more exacting. It is the difference between a book that merely contains text and a book that has been designed to be read.

That difference is usually not dramatic on any single page. It lives in accumulation. The type sits comfortably. The margins feel intentional. Chapter openings have authority. Scene breaks look considered rather than improvised. Front matter belongs to the same book as the body pages. Nothing is shouting for attention, and nothing feels accidental. Good interior design is rarely flashy. It is controlled.

A manuscript is not a finished book interior

A manuscript file can be clean, edited, and perfectly readable on screen while still looking unfinished in print. Word-processing defaults are built for drafting and revision, not for book design. They do not know whether the book is a literary novel, a commercial romance, a practical nonfiction guide, or an illustrated art title. They do not know whether the trim is 5.5 x 8.5, whether the book will be printed on cream stock, whether the chapters are short and numerous, or whether the author needs generous margins because the page is dense with text.

That is where formatting becomes design rather than clerical cleanup. A formatter is not simply pressing “justify” and inserting page numbers. The job is to decide how the book should behave on the page and then execute that consistently across the whole interior.

What professional formatting actually changes

Most readers could not give you a technical breakdown of why one interior feels polished and another feels amateur. They can still feel the difference. Usually they are responding to a cluster of small decisions made well—or made badly.

  • Typeface selection. The type needs to suit the category, the trim size, and the reading experience. A face that looks elegant in a chapter heading may be miserable in a 90,000-word novel if the colour on the page is too dark or the italics are weak.
  • Margins and text block. A page with timid outer margins and a swollen text block often feels cramped even if nothing is technically “wrong.” Conversely, pages that are too airy can look underdesigned or juvenile. Good interiors find the right balance for the trim and genre.
  • Leading, paragraph treatment, and rhythm. Line spacing, first-line indents, spacing before headings, treatment of scene breaks—these are rhythm decisions. They determine whether the pages feel settled or fussy.
  • Hierarchy. Front matter, title page, chapter openers, subheads, captions, extracts, ornamental breaks, running heads, and folios all need a clear relationship to one another. If every element feels as though it came from a different document, the reader notices even if they cannot articulate why.
  • Widows, orphans, bad breaks, and visual interruptions. A professional interior is not just globally styled; it is checked page by page. Strange line breaks, stacked hyphens, awkward page turns, isolated lines, and clumsy chapter endings all chip away at the impression of care.
  • Consistency. This is the quiet one, but it matters. If chapter titles, ornamental breaks, extracted quotations, footnotes, and image captions all obey the same visual logic, the book feels trustworthy. If they do not, it feels homemade.

Beautiful interiors are usually restrained

When people say a book has a beautiful interior, they are not usually praising decoration. They are responding to proportion and discipline. A well-made novel may look almost plain at first glance. The elegance is in the fit: the right type at the right size, enough air around the text, chapter openings that feel deliberate, and pages that do not fight the prose.

That restraint is part of why professional formatting is easy to undervalue. Readers notice bad interiors immediately—crowded margins, ugly chapter breaks, inconsistent spacing, pages that feel grey and oppressive—but they often do not notice good interiors as a separate achievement. They simply experience the book as competent, readable, and worth taking seriously. That is exactly the point.

Different books need different interiors

This is where templated formatting often falls apart. A one-size-fits-all interior can be serviceable, but it rarely feels fully right because books do not ask the same things of the page.

A Victorian-style romance may benefit from slightly more formal chapter treatment than a contemporary thriller. A practical nonfiction book may need a very different hierarchy for subheads, lists, pull quotes, and tables. A heavily illustrated art book is an entirely different production problem from a dialogue-heavy commercial novel. Even within fiction, a short, airy manuscript and a dense 140,000-word historical are not going to want the same margins, line length, or typographic texture.

Professional formatting begins by asking what sort of object the book is trying to be. The interior should not look generic. It should look appropriate.

Formatting is also about trust

Readers make judgments quickly. So do reviewers, booksellers, librarians, and industry people. A weak interior may not stop a determined reader from finishing a good story, but it does signal something: that the final stage of production was treated as an afterthought, or that no one involved quite knew how a finished book should look.

That may sound severe, but books are physical design objects as well as containers for text. If the interior feels careless, the reader is more likely to assume other parts of the process were careless too. The inverse is also true. A calm, well-built interior suggests editorial seriousness, technical competence, and respect for the work.

LT Publishing’s view: formatting should do more than clear a technical hurdle. If a book is worth printing, it is worth making readable, proportionate, and visually coherent. That does not mean turning every novel into an ornamented special edition. It means treating the interior as part of the book, not as an administrative afterthought.

So what does a formatter actually do?

At the practical level, professional interior work usually includes establishing page dimensions and margins, choosing and styling type, building master paragraph styles, setting chapter architecture, designing front matter, controlling scene-break treatment, preparing running heads and folios where appropriate, cleaning up spacing and bad breaks, and producing a final print-ready PDF that behaves correctly when it goes to press.

At the less visible level, it also includes judgment. Is the page too grey? Are the chapter openings overdesigned? Does the body font feel brittle? Are the margins generous enough for the trim? Is the book long enough that running heads would help, or short enough that they would only clutter it? Does the typography match the tone of the text? Those are not template questions. They are book questions.

When professional formatting matters most

Not every project needs elaborate design, but almost every print book benefits from someone who understands interiors as books rather than as documents. That becomes especially important when:

  • the book is intended to look like a serious trade title rather than a self-published draft in book form;
  • the manuscript is long, dense, or typographically awkward;
  • the project includes images, extracts, notes, lists, tables, or other elements beyond straightforward running text;
  • the author wants the printed book to feel consistent with a professionally designed cover; or
  • the file will be sold widely enough that presentation itself becomes part of the book’s credibility.

In other words: professional formatting matters whenever the author wants the interior to feel intentional rather than merely acceptable.

The short version

Some books have beautiful interiors because someone designed them as books. Not as Word documents. Not as a last-minute upload requirement. Not as an afterthought once the cover was done. As books.

That does not require fussiness, and it does not require extravagance. It requires judgment, proportion, and enough technical competence to make the pages behave. Readers may never stop and say, “This was expertly formatted.” But they will feel the result. The book will look calmer, read better, and carry itself like something finished.
